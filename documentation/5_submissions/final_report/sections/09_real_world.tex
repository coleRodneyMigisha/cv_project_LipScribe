\section{Real-World Speech}
\label{sec:realworld}

\subsection{Test sets and protocol}
The real-world models are scored on two held-out test sets: 1,094 TED clips from 67~talks, and
41~film clips from three films. No test talk or film was used for training, validation or the
language models. The references are Whisper large-v3-turbo transcripts of each clip's own audio
(Section~\ref{sec:pipelines-real}), so TED and film WER here measure agreement with Whisper. The
film set is small, and its confidence intervals are correspondingly wide. Noise is the fixed
MUSAN test mixture at 0, $-5$ and $-10$~dB. Each test set was scored once, after the decoder had
been chosen on validation. Differences between two models come with a 95~\% paired bootstrap
interval (10,000 resamples of whole talks for TED and of clips for films).

\subsection{The lips help in noise}
Table~\ref{tab:realworld} compares the warm start ws\_v5 with its audio-only twin twin\_v5. On
clean speech the two are close. On TED, ws\_v5 is 1.3~points worse, and the interval excludes
zero; on films they tie. As the noise grows, the lips matter more. At $-5$~dB, ws\_v5 is
4.9~points better on TED and 11.3~points better on films. At $-10$~dB, it cuts TED WER from
42.3~\% to 28.5~\% and film WER from 64.5~\% to 49.2~\%, and both intervals exclude zero.
Beam search with the LM gains 1 to 3~points over greedy decoding for both models. ws\_v5 is
therefore the real-world model. Its small cost on clean TED speech is the price of a gate that
trusts the lips when the sound is poor.

\subsection{From the first fusion to ws\_v5}
Each fusion design in Section~\ref{sec:pipelines-real} was kept or dropped by comparing it with
the audio-only twin on the same test set. On the first clips, with greedy decoding, the twin
scored 17.4~\% on clean TED and 43.8~\% at $-10$~dB. The lip-anchored fusion reached 36.2~\% at
$-10$~dB, but 26.9~\% on clean speech, so it traded clean accuracy for accuracy in noise. The
reliability-gated fusion, trained from scratch, kept most of both (18.5~\% clean, 32.9~\% at
$-10$~dB). The warm start then matched the twin on clean speech (17.2~\% against 17.4~\%) and
reached 31.4~\% at $-10$~dB (\texttt{reports/flix\_v4/decision\_log.md} and
\texttt{reports/flix\_v5/decision\_log.md}).

Re-cutting the clips with 0.32~s of padding (v5) helped less than we expected. On the re-cut TED
test set, ws\_v5 beat the v4 warm start by 0.7~points on clean speech (interval $-1.3$ to
$-0.2$) and by 1.5~points at $-10$~dB. However, the success criterion set before the run was a
gain of at least one point on clean speech and on the mean over noise levels, so the re-cut fell
short of it. Edge words also stayed hard: ws\_v5 still got a clip's first or last word wrong
30.6~\% of the time, against 10.8~\% for inner words, so they failed the target of less than
1.5~times the inner-word error.

\subsection{Audio that does not belong to the face: a negative result}
In real use, the voice may not belong to the face on screen. The speaker may only mouth words,
someone else may talk over them, or someone else may talk instead. ws\_v5 never saw such audio
in training, because its noise is non-speech only. Table~\ref{tab:wrongaudio} shows the result.
When another person's speech replaces the speaker's, ws\_v5 transcribes the wrong voice. Its TED
WER then reaches 103.3~\%, far worse than reading the lips alone: lips\_v5, which is ws\_v5 with
its audio stream switched off, scores 42.3~\%.

We therefore trained ws\_v6, the same warm start with interference added to 35~\% of the
training clips: silence (10~\%), another clip's speech alone (10~\%) and talk-over at
$-5$ to $10$~dB (15~\%), the label always being the on-screen speaker's words. On wrong audio it
behaved as hoped. It matched lip reading when the voice was replaced (42.3~\% against 42.3~\% on
TED) and beat ws\_v5 under talk-over (39.0~\% against 57.7~\%). It also lost 15.9~points on
clean TED speech (32.8~\% against 16.9~\%), however, far beyond the one point we had allowed. Its
gate cannot tell a matching voice from a mismatched one using the features alone, so it trusts
the audio less everywhere. ws\_v5 therefore remains the real-world model. Detecting a mismatched
voice needs an explicit audio-visual synchrony signal, which we leave to future work
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The demo uses ws\_v5's lips only where a segment shows one
frontal, speaking face (Section~\ref{sec:application}).
